# CS-250: Software Development Lifecycle

This repository holds my portfolio artifact from CS-250 at Southern New Hampshire University: the Sprint Review and Retrospective from the course's final project. In the project, I played different roles on a Scrum team building a booking tool for SNHU Travel, a fictional travel agency client. The retrospective is written as the team's Scrum Master and looks back on what each role contributed, how the user stories got finished, and how the team handled a mid-project change from the client.

## Reflection

### How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating user stories help with this?

I start with what users actually say, then turn each request into something small enough to build and test. In the SNHU Travel focus group, one customer asked for "the top 5 or 10 destinations listed." That's a real need, but it isn't a feature yet. Writing it as a user story, something like "As a customer, I want to see the top destinations so I can pick a trip faster," forces three questions: who wants it, what they want, and why. The "why" is the part that matters when requirements change. When the client switched the whole product to detox and wellness travel, the story's goal stayed the same. Only the content changed, so I swapped the slide show over to five wellness destinations instead of rebuilding it. User stories also give the tester something concrete to check, because every story should have acceptance criteria that say exactly when it's done.

### How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?

I build in small pieces and run the program early, because reading code only tells me so much. On my slide show, the description bar was hidden under the image because of an inherited layout problem, and I only caught it by running the program. That taught me to get feedback while the code is still fresh instead of waiting until I think I'm done. The Agile processes I'd bring with me are the ones I argued for during the course. Story points with Planning Poker give a team realistic estimates without padding hours. Pair programming with rotating pairs keeps one person from being the only one who understands part of the code. A short Definition of Done, written together, makes everyone agree on what finished means before work starts. I'd also bring testers in from the start of each sprint, since that was the biggest lesson from my own retrospective.

### What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

To me, a good team member makes it easy for other people to do their part. When the SNHU Travel client changed direction, I sent the Product Owner and tester one email that split my questions by person, gave each a deadline, and said what I'd do if I didn't hear back. Nobody had to guess what I needed. In a group discussion about the Vision Quest case study, I wrote up our final plan so every teammate had at least one of their ideas in it, and I ended by asking if it worked for everyone. Being a good teammate also means asking before building, like checking who writes the acceptance criteria so I'm not guessing. And it means owning the mistakes that show up in a retrospective, because the point of a retrospective is getting better as a team, not looking good as an individual.
